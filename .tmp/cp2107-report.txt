1     Overview, Question, and Scope
Offline learning for language-model agents is usually trained with a causal autoregressive fac-
torisation: given a left-to-right history, the model is supervised to predict the next token or
next action. That objective is simple and effective, but it uses only one conditional view of each
trajectory. A single interaction trace also contains other conditional learning problems: predict-
ing the next observation from the past, inferring the action that caused a state change, filling
in missing intermediate steps, retrodicting earlier state from later consequences, and repairing
corrupted observations.
Masked Trajectory Models (MTM) [1] and UniMASK [2] show that, in sequential decision
problems, different masks over the same trajectory instantiate different inference tasks. This
proposal studies whether the same principle can be made useful for text-agent trajectories when
the substrate is a masked diffusion language model (MDLM) [3, 4]. The core object of study is
not diffusion language modelling in general; it is the masking strategy used to train the diffusion
backbone.
The central hypothesis is narrow: flexible, block-wise, heteromodal masking over text-agent tra-
jectories should produce more transferable world-model-like representations than causal masking
or naive random masking, when the diffusion substrate and supervision budget are held fixed.
The project does not claim to solve general world modelling. It tests a limited claim in text en-
vironments with explicit, verifiable dynamics: ScienceWorld [14], ALFWorld [13], and WebShop
[15]. The decisive comparison is between two models using the same diffusion checkpoint and
the same optimisation budget. D-AR uses a diffusion backbone restricted to causal prefix-to-
suffix conditioning. D-Flex uses the same backbone but trains on a mixture of forward, inverse,
retrodictive, imputation, and denoising masks over typed trajectory blocks. If D-Flex improves
out-of-distribution dynamics prediction, latent state recovery, and counterfactual consistency
relative to D-AR, the evidence points to the masking strategy rather than merely to the diffusion
substrate.

1.1   Research Questions
 1. Does flexible block-wise masking on a masked diffusion language model produce a smaller
    in-distribution to out-of-distribution generalisation gap than causal masking on the same
    diffusion substrate?

 2. Does flexible masking improve action-conditioned next-state prediction, counterfactual con-
    sistency, and latent-state decodability compared with causal masking and naive random
    masking?

 3. Is any observed gain attributable specifically to masking and reconstructing environmental
    observations, rather than to extra supervision, easier targets, longer context, or the diffusion
    backbone itself?

 4. How do the effects differ across environment types: scientific rule dynamics in ScienceWorld,
    household physical dynamics in ALFWorld, and attribute/search dynamics in WebShop?

1.2   Operational Definition
In this proposal, an implicit text-world model is not defined as a complete representation of the
external world. It is defined operationally. A model has a stronger implicit text-world model for
a given environment if, under held-out configurations and interventions, it satisfies the following
criteria better than matched baselines:

 • it predicts action-conditioned next observations and state variables from interaction history;

 • it maintains coherent latent representations of task-relevant state, such as inventory, object
   locations, achieved subgoals, and catalogue constraints;

 • it responds correctly to valid counterfactual edits, such as removing a prerequisite object or
   changing a rule condition;

 • it recovers from corrupted or inconsistent trajectory fragments without peeking at future
   ground truth;

 • it improves or preserves task success on out-of-distribution splits.

This definition keeps the claim testable. A positive result would show evidence for world-model-
like structure in a controlled text-agent setting. It would not show that the model has a general,
human-like, or open-ended model of the world.

1.3   Positioning
Masking as multi-task supervision. MTM [1] reconstructs masked state, action, and re-
turn tokens from arbitrary subsets of a trajectory, allowing the same network to act as a forward
dynamics model, inverse dynamics model, and policy. UniMASK [2] frames behaviour cloning,
offline reinforcement learning, inverse dynamics, and waypoint conditioning as different masking
patterns over a shared sequence model. Fill-in-the-middle training [8] shows that even autore-
gressive systems can benefit from nonstandard infilling factorisations, though causal attention
limits the available conditioning structure. This project adapts the masking-as-multitask idea
to typed text-agent trajectories.

Masked diffusion language models. MDLM [3] reduces discrete masked diffusion training
to a Rao-Blackwellised mixture of masked-language-modelling losses. LLaDA [4] scales diffusion
language modelling to large transformer backbones with strong bidirectional behaviour. ProS-
eCo [9] studies correction during masked diffusion generation, addressing the problem that once
tokens are unmasked, standard masked diffusion samplers can accumulate errors. This project
uses masked diffusion because denoising and bidirectional reconstruction are natural tools for
probing trajectory consistency. It does not assume that diffusion models are already good agent
backbones.




                                                 2
Implicit world models in sequence models. Othello-GPT [5] and follow-up probe work
[6] show that sequence models can learn latent state representations in synthetic domains.
Recent work on text-based world models [10] studies when language models can serve as next-
state predictors in interactive text environments. At the same time, diffusion language models
have shown weaknesses in agentic workflows: recent evaluations report failures in branching
under temporal feedback and in preserving symbolic precision [11]. This proposal therefore
asks a targeted question: can trajectory-specific masking supply world-model supervision that
a generic diffusion language model lacks?

2.2   Trajectory Data
Primary trajectories are drawn from released ETO trajectories [12] and compatible public agent-
trajectory corpora where available. The project reuses existing trajectories rather than generat-
ing a large new interaction dataset. This keeps the focus on the training objective and masking
strategy.
Each trajectory is represented as a sequence of typed semantic blocks:

where g is the task or goal specification and each block bi has a type in

                                {Think, Action, Observation}.

When simulator metadata is available, the trajectory is also aligned to structured state labels
such as inventory, object locations, achieved subgoals, current room, product attributes, or valid
action preconditions. These labels are never used as generation targets in the main language-
model loss; they are used for evaluation probes and state-level metrics.

2.3   Leakage Controls
Reasoning traces can leak future state if they were generated after the trajectory or if they
contain summaries of later observations. Therefore, all Think blocks are audited by position.
The main experiments include Think blocks only when they are temporally valid. A dedicated
ablation compares training with Think blocks retained, masked, and removed. State probes
are evaluated on histories where the probed variable is not trivially present in the immediately
visible observation text.

5.1   Baseline Objectives as Restrictions
D-AR restricts C to causal prefixes and R to suffix or next-block targets. It therefore cannot
condition on future observations when predicting earlier actions or states. If suffix tokens are
predicted in parallel, D-AR is not exactly a standard autoregressive factorisation; it is a causal
conditional denoiser. This distinction is reported explicitly. The external AR-SFT and AR-
FullSeq baselines provide true causal autoregressive references.
D-Random samples target tokens independently and uniformly, with no typed block constraints
and no per-type masking propensities. Its unmasked tokens form bidirectional context, so
it is a strong generic masked baseline rather than an empty-context baseline. D-Flex differs
from D-Random by using semantic blocks, environment/action/observation types, curriculum
scheduling, and explicit objective mixtures.


6     Supervision and Compute Controls
Flexible masking can turn one trajectory into many supervised views. Without controls, D-
Flex could win simply by receiving more loss-bearing targets. The primary matched-supervision
regime holds the following quantities fixed across conditions wherever possible:

                                                  6
 • the pool of unique trajectories;

 • total gradient steps;

 • batch token count;

 • expected number of loss-bearing token positions per trajectory per epoch;

 • target-type proportions across Action, Observation, Think, and Goal blocks;

 • context-length distribution;

 • evaluation prompts and decoding budget.

For a trajectory of length L, an autoregressive full-sequence pass supervises approximately L
positions. A flexible masked view with expected target fraction r̄ supervises approximately r̄L
positions. With k resampled views per trajectory, matched full-sequence supervision sets

                                             kr̄ ≈ 1.

For example, if r̄ = 0.33, then k ≈ 3. For an action-only baseline, the comparable target count
is the action-token fraction rather than the full sequence length. This is why AR-FullSeq is
reported alongside AR-SFT.
Equal target count still does not guarantee equal information content. Observation tokens may
be easier or harder than action tokens, future context may reduce entropy, and some targets
may be nearly templated. Therefore the analysis reports target entropy proxies, block-type
proportions, mean context length, and reconstruction difficulty by block type. The primary
D-AR versus D-Flex comparison is interpreted together with these diagnostics.


7     Incremental Masking Curriculum
D-Flex uses an incremental masking curriculum. Training begins with low target ratios, around
10 percent, where reconstruction is local and stable. The target ratio then ramps toward higher
ratios, around 50 to 70 percent, where the model must use longer-range structure rather than
nearby token copying.
The endpoint is empirical rather than assumed. The experiment reports performance at 30,
50, and 70 percent ceilings, along with reconstruction loss, OOD dynamics metrics, and failure
modes. A fixed-ratio baseline is included to test whether the curriculum itself matters or whether
the final mask distribution is sufficient.


8     Evaluation Plan
8.1   Task Success: ID and OOD
Task success is measured on in-distribution and out-of-distribution splits for ScienceWorld,
ALFWorld, and WebShop. The main behavioural metric is the OOD gap:

                                      ∆OOD = SID − SOOD ,

where S is the environment-specific success or reward score. A smaller OOD gap is evidence
of better transfer only when absolute OOD performance is also competitive. A model that
performs poorly everywhere can have a small gap for the wrong reason.


                                                7
8.2   Action-Conditioned Next-State Prediction
The primary world-model metric is action-conditioned next-state prediction. Given history ht
and action at , the model predicts the next observation or structured state:
                                      ôt+1 ∼ pθ (ot+1 | ht , at ).
Evaluation compares predicted observations to simulator observations and, where available,
predicted structured state variables to ground-truth state labels. Text similarity is not sufficient;
the analysis prioritises state-level correctness, valid preconditions, object changes, and rule-
consistent consequences.
Closed-loop rollouts evaluate horizons 3, 5, and 10. The model’s own predicted observations are
fed back into subsequent prediction steps. No future ground-truth observation is used as context
during closed-loop evaluation. This prevents inpainting from being mistaken for simulation.

8.3   Counterfactual Consistency
Counterfactual tests introduce valid off-manifold edits and ask whether predicted dynamics
change appropriately. Examples include removing a prerequisite object in ScienceWorld, moving
a household object in ALFWorld, or altering a product constraint in WebShop. The edited state
is supplied as context, and the model must predict the consequence of an action under the edited
state.
Counterfactuals are generated through environment-valid transformations whenever possible.
Invalid or ambiguous edits are excluded. This matters because a model should not be penalised
for failing to resolve a counterfactual that the simulator itself does not define.

8.4   Latent-State Probes
Linear probes are trained on frozen hidden activations to decode ground-truth state variables.
Probe targets include inventory, object location, achieved subgoals, physical preconditions, cat-
alogue constraints, and task-relevant flags. Probe inputs are taken from histories where the
target variable is not trivially visible in the final observation string.
Probe baselines include bag-of-words features, frozen pretrained checkpoints before trajectory
fine-tuning, shuffled labels, and layer-wise comparisons. Probe results are interpreted as diag-
nostic evidence, not proof. High linear decodability can indicate state tracking, but it can also
reflect textual shortcuts.

8.5   Corruption Recovery and ProSeCo-Style Correction
The corruption-recovery evaluation injects impossible observations, invalid actions, deleted
steps, and inconsistent state descriptions into trajectories. Models are scored by recovery rate,
steps to return to coherent dynamics, and post-corruption rollout quality.
Standard iterative diffusion decoding is compared with one-shot mask prediction and ProSeCo-
style corrective refinement. ProSeCo-style correction is treated as a decoding and sampling
ablation, not as part of the core D-Flex claim. If correction improves D-Flex, the analysis
separates gains from training masks and gains from inference-time revision.

8.6   Dataset-Specific Analysis
Each environment receives both shared metrics and type-specific analyses. ScienceWorld anal-
ysis focuses on rule consistency, scientific preconditions, and unseen rule combinations. ALF-
World analysis focuses on object permanence, location tracking, receptacle relations, and action

                                                   8
affordances. WebShop analysis focuses on attribute constraints, product-ranking state, search
refinement, and constraint satisfaction.
Results are reported per dataset, per task type, and in aggregate. Aggregates use normalised
metrics so that reward scales from different environments do not dominate one another. A
positive result must not depend on a single dataset hiding failures in the others.

10     Analysis and Statistical Reporting
The primary comparison is D-Flex versus D-AR on the same diffusion checkpoint. Secondary
comparisons include D-Flex versus D-Random, D-Flex versus AR-FullSeq, and D-Flex versus
AR-SFT. Results are reported with mean and uncertainty across random seeds where compute
permits. If compute prevents full replication for the largest runs, those results are labelled
exploratory and smaller-scale replicated runs are used to estimate variance.
The analysis reports both behavioural and mechanistic outcomes:
 • ID and OOD task success;
 • action-conditioned next-state accuracy;
 • closed-loop rollout coherence;
 • counterfactual consistency;
 • latent-state probe accuracy;
 • corruption recovery;
 • target-type reconstruction loss;
 • compute, tokens, and wall-clock cost.
An outcome is considered strong evidence for the proposal only if D-Flex improves the world-
model diagnostics and not merely the easiest reconstruction losses. An outcome where D-Flex
improves probes but not task success is still informative: it would suggest that representation
learning improved while decoding, planning, or action selection remained the bottleneck.


11     Success Criteria
The strongest positive result is:
 • D-Flex beats D-AR on OOD task success or narrows the OOD gap while preserving ID
   performance;
 • D-Flex improves action-conditioned next-state prediction and closed-loop rollout coherence;
 • D-Flex improves counterfactual consistency under environment-valid edits;
 • D-Flex improves latent-state probe performance under anti-leakage controls;
 • observation-masking ablations show that the gains disappear or shrink when observations
   are removed from the reconstruction targets.
A clean negative result is also valuable. If D-Flex matches D-AR under matched supervision and
context controls, then flexible masking adds little beyond causal diffusion training. If D-Random
matches D-Flex, then structured block-wise heteromodal masking is unnecessary. If AR-FullSeq
matches or beats D-Flex on world-model metrics, then causal full-sequence modelling may be
sufficient for these environments.


12     Known Limitations
Limited world scope. The proposal studies text-agent environments with explicit state and
verifiable dynamics. It does not establish open-ended world modelling, physical grounding
beyond text, or general scientific reasoning.

                                               10
Base-model confounds. AR-SFT and diffusion backbones cannot share initialisation. There-
fore AR-versus-diffusion comparisons are softer than D-AR-versus-D-Flex comparisons. The
within-substrate diffusion comparison is the primary causal contrast.

Supervision composition. Matched target count does not equal matched information. D-
Flex may see richer target mixtures and future context. The AR-FullSeq control, block-type
reporting, context-length controls, and action-only/observation-only ablations are included to
reduce this ambiguity.

Templated observations. ScienceWorld and ALFWorld observations can contain repeated
templates. A model may reconstruct plausible text without learning causal dynamics. This is
why state-level next-observation metrics and counterfactual tests are primary.

Offline coverage. Expert trajectories may undersample failures, invalid actions, and rare
state transitions. This limits counterfactual learning. Where possible, held-out interventions
are generated through environment simulators rather than inferred only from expert traces.

Probe dependence. Linear probes diagnose representational accessibility, not necessarily
causal use. Probe results are paired with closed-loop prediction and counterfactual interventions
to avoid overclaiming.

Execution risk. Running all datasets, conditions, ablations, and probe analyses is compute-
intensive. The plan therefore separates the primary D-AR versus D-Flex test from secondary
ablations in reporting order, but the intended experimental suite includes all ablations and all
dataset comparisons.


13     Infrastructure
Training and activation collection run on the NUS SOC GPU cluster via Slurm. The minimum
target is one A100 40GB for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, with A100 80GB or H100 jobs
reserved for activation collection, long-context batches, and full ablation runs. Diffusion con-
ditions fine-tune one open masked-diffusion backbone such as LLaDA2.0-mini. Autoregressive
baselines use Ling-mini-2.0.
The implementation includes explicit logging of trajectory IDs, mask objective IDs, target block
types, context length, target ratio, diffusion time, random seed, and supervised token count.
These logs are required for the supervision-budget audit.


14     Timeline
The full plan is a ten-week sprint beginning in June 2026. Because the scope includes all datasets
and all ablations, the schedule is aggressive and depends on cluster availability. The reporting
order is pre-registered so that the primary scientific comparison is available even if reruns are
delayed.
